Victim-survivor vulnerability

Victim-Survivors

Victim-survivors are not responsible for the offender's decision to distribute or threaten to distribute images. Vulnerability often comes from trust, intimacy, coercion, and unequal power in relationships, not from wrongdoing by the victim.

Who Is Most Vulnerable?

Shapiro (2023) identifies female adolescents and young adults, especially ages 15–29, as common victims of nonconsensual pornography dissemination. Wolak and Finkelhor (2016) also found that their sextortion survey respondents were mostly female, with many incidents beginning during the teenage years or early adulthood.

Some victim-survivors face added vulnerability when they fear judgment, family rejection, discrimination, or exposure of their sexual orientation, gender identity, or private relationships.

Connection to Abusive Relationships

In abusive relationships, nonconsensual image distribution can function as a form of power and control. An offender may threaten exposure to force reconciliation, prevent a breakup, punish independence, damage the victim's reputation, or keep the victim silent. This connects to the power wheel because the abuse is not only sexual or digital; it can also involve intimidation, emotional abuse, isolation, coercion, and threats.

What Places Victims in the Path of the Offender?

Victims may be targeted after sharing images in trust, using social media, communicating through dating apps, storing images in cloud accounts, or trying to leave a controlling relationship.

Why Victims May Stay Silent

Shame, embarrassment, self-blame, fear of not being believed, and fear that family, school, employers, or police will judge them may keep victim-survivors from reporting or seeking support.
