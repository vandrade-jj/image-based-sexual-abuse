Legal response

Laws

Nonconsensual distribution of intimate images can involve criminal, civil, and platform-based responses. Laws can help, but online image abuse can be difficult to prove when evidence disappears or when multiple jurisdictions are involved.

New York Example

  • New York criminalizes the unlawful dissemination or publication of an intimate image.
  • The basic issue is whether an identifiable person's private intimate image was shared without consent when that person had a reasonable expectation that it would remain private.
  • New York is a helpful example, but victims should check the law in their own state because legal definitions and consequences vary.
  • For state-by-state information, visitors can start with the Without My Consent 50 State Project: https://withoutmyconsent.org/50state/

Acts Needed to Prove the Crime

  • The offender distributed, posted, shared, published, or threatened to publish an intimate image.
  • The image showed an identifiable person.
  • The image was private or intimate in nature.
  • The victim did not consent to the distribution, even if the image was originally created or shared privately with consent.

Intention Needed to Prove the Crime

  • The offender acted intentionally or knowingly, depending on the law used.
  • The offender knew or should have understood that the victim did not consent to the image being shared.
  • Some laws may require proof that the offender intended to harm, harass, threaten, coerce, or cause emotional distress.
  • Proving intention can be difficult if the offender uses anonymous accounts, deletes messages, or claims someone else posted the image.

Are Current Laws Sufficient?

  • Current laws are helpful because they recognize that consent to create or privately send an image is not consent to distribute it.
  • However, current laws are not always sufficient because state definitions are inconsistent and online abuse can cross multiple jurisdictions.
  • Evidence can disappear quickly if accounts, posts, or messages are deleted before they are documented.
  • It may be difficult to prove who posted the image when private groups, anonymous usernames, shared devices, or overseas platforms are involved.
  • Civil remedies may also be available. Without My Consent lists possible New York common-law claims such as intentional infliction of emotional distress, conspiracy, conversion, and privacy-related claims, depending on the facts.
